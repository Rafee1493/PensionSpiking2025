1. Executive summary

Pension spiking — the strategic escalation of overtime earnings in the years immediately preceding retirement to inflate the Final Average Salary (FAS) — is a well-documented vulnerability in public-sector defined-benefit systems. This report presents quantitative evidence of systematic spiking in two New York PFRS agencies: the NYS Division of Police and the Suffolk County Police Department.

Out of 483 retirees in 2024 across the two agencies, 382 were reliably matched to payroll records, of which 340 drew overtime pay and form the analytical sample. Among this group, retirees increased their average overtime by 93% at the State Police and 82% at Suffolk County during their final three years — substantially above the same-period growth seen among active officers in equivalent bargaining units.

Overtime added an average of 28% to the State Police retirees' final three years of pay and 34% to the Suffolk County retirees' — translating into roughly $20,000 in extra annual pension for State Police retirees and $39,000 for Suffolk retirees, paid for life with cost-of-living adjustments.

A quarter of the sampled workers — 17% of NYSP retirees and 46% of Suffolk retirees — boosted their pensions enough to exceed their final base salaries. Extrapolating to the full PFRS, this scale of spiking adds tens of millions of dollars to retirement system costs annually for each new class of retirees, and hundreds of millions over the duration of their pension-collecting years.

2. Background: the pension spiking mechanism

The New York Police and Fire Retirement System (PFRS) has four member tiers. Tiers 1 and 2 cover officers who joined before July 1, 2009. Tier 3 covers a brief window from July 2009 through March 2012. Tier 6 applies to officers hired on or after April 1, 2012. There is no Tier 4 or Tier 5 in PFRS — those tiers exist only in the separate Employees' Retirement System.

Under PFRS Tier 2 — the dominant tier in the 2024 retiree cohort — a member's pension equals their Final Average Salary (the average of the three highest consecutive years of earnings) multiplied by years of credited service and a tier-specific accrual rate. Overtime is included in FAS without any cap. Tier 5 and 6 members face a 15% of salary cap on pensionable overtime, but represent fewer than 2% of 2024 retirees.

For the State Police and Suffolk County PD, the formula is parallel: 2% of FAS for the first 25 years of service, plus 1.67% for each additional year, capped at 70%. So a Trooper with a $100,000 base salary who adds $40,000 per year in overtime during their three-year FAS window raises their FAS to $140,000 — translating, at 25 years of service, to a $20,000 per year pension uplift for life.

The 20% anti-spiking rule caps year-over-year FAS growth at 20%. Any earnings exceeding 120% of the prior year's earnings are excluded. This creates a strategic ramp: rather than spiking in a single final year, officers gradually escalate overtime over 3–4 years — staying within the 20% threshold each year while substantially inflating FAS.

3. Data & methodology

3.1 Data sources and sample construction

The analysis began with 483 employees who retired in 2024 from NYSP and SCPD. Retirees were matched by name to payroll records covering 2018–2024. After excluding name-mismatch cases and duplicates, 382 retirees were reliably matched, of which 340 drew overtime pay and entered the analysis. This breaks down into 206 NYSP retirees and 134 SCPD retirees.

A control group of non-retiring employees was also constructed — 2,785 from NYSP and 1,347 from SCPD — drawn from the same agencies, bargaining units, and salary ranges as the retiree sample.

3.2 Overtime intensity metric and spike window definition

We normalize overtime using the overtime intensity ratio: annual overtime earnings divided by the officer's annualized base pay rate. Three-year averages are computed for the baseline window (2018–2020) and a spike window. Critically, the spike window is defined differently depending on when the officer retired:

  • Officers retiring in the second half of 2024 (July–December): spike window is 2022–2024, with 2024 OT annualized.
  • Officers retiring in the first half of 2024 (January–June): spike window is 2021–2023, since the FAS window for these officers is more likely to include 2021 than 2024.

For the control group, the 2022–2024 window is used uniformly. Only officers with non-zero overtime in both periods are included.

3.3 Suffolk County gross pay adjustment

Suffolk County payroll data reports gross annual pay — total compensation including overtime and other supplements — rather than a fixed base rate. Several Suffolk officers (e.g., Michael Malerba and Eric Denis) worked their highest overtime several years before retirement, which depresses their spike-window intensity ratio while inflating their gross pay in those earlier years. As a result, the Suffolk DiD estimates likely understate the true magnitude of pension spiking. To correct for this in pension-vs-pay comparisons (Section 6.2), we use each officer's average gross pay over 2021–2023 as the baseline.

3.4 Difference-in-differences design

The DiD estimator asks: did retirees' OT intensity increase more than comparable active officers' intensity over the same period? The parallel trends assumption is supported by both groups facing identical labor market conditions, COVID staffing pressures, and contract cycles.

Conservative bias: Active employees within 1–3 years of retirement are themselves likely already spiking. Their inclusion in the control group means the DiD estimate understates the true magnitude of spiking — the actual gap between genuine non-spikers and retirees is larger than reported here.

4. Core evidence: difference-in-differences results

Both retirees and non-retirees were paid for higher amounts of overtime, on average, in the second three-year period. However, the increase for employees on the verge of retiring was 25 percentage points higher than the increase for employees who remained active. This pattern held for almost every sub-group, with one exception: in the Suffolk County detectives' bargaining unit, the rate of increase for retirees was 5 points smaller than for active employees.

What the results show

Five of six bargaining units show retirees outpacing active officers, with the gap reaching 22 to 34 percentage points in the State Police and 21 to 28 in Suffolk County. Suffolk County's PBA patrol officers — the largest single subgroup at 81 retirees — show the highest absolute OT intensity, with retirees averaging 42% of base pay in overtime during the spike window versus 21% in baseline.

The Suffolk County detectives are the one subgroup with no detectable spiking signal: active detectives outpaced retirees by 8 points. Two factors plausibly explain this. First, detectives' overtime is often case-driven (court appearances, ongoing investigations) and may be less amenable to strategic accumulation. Second, detective base pay is structurally higher, so the marginal pension gain from a dollar of overtime is somewhat smaller than for patrol officers.

6. Pension cost impact

Overtime earns its outsized fiscal significance not as a one-time payroll cost but as a permanent uplift to FAS, which then pays out for the rest of the retiree's life with annual cost-of-living adjustments.

6.1 The fiscal exposure

The average pension boost from spiking — the portion of each retiree's annual pension attributable to OT increases above what control-group officers experienced — is approximately $7,600 per retiree per year. Across all 340 matched retirees, this totals roughly $2.6 million per year in excess pension obligations.

Assuming each retiree lives 20 years past retirement, with cost-of-living adjustments rising at 2% annually, the pension spiking observed in this single 2024 cohort imposes an estimated lifetime cost of ~$47 million on New York taxpayers.

If extrapolated across the full PFRS — covering all police and fire retirees statewide — these averages indicate that spiking adds tens of millions of dollars annually to system costs for each new class of retirees, and hundreds of millions over the course of their retirement years. The 15% OT cap that applies to Tier 5 and 6 members would, if applied to this 2024 cohort, have reduced the magnitude of spiking by roughly 37% for State Police and 52% for Suffolk County.

6.2 Pensions vs. pay: how many retirees earn more in pension than in salary?

For NYSP, each retiree's pension is compared against their 2023 base pay rate. For Suffolk County, pensions are compared against each officer's average gross pay over 2021–2023 — necessary because Suffolk payroll reports gross compensation (inclusive of overtime), not a fixed pay rate. Officers like Michael Malerba and Eric Denis earned very high overtime in 2019–2021 but far less in 2022–23, making any single-year gross-pay comparison misleading. The 2021–2023 average is more representative.

Even with this conservative adjustment, 61 of 134 Suffolk retirees (46%) collect an annual pension exceeding their 2023 gross pay. For State Police, 34 of 206 retirees (17%) collect pensions above their 2023 base pay rate.

8. Contextual benchmarks

This analysis is consistent with prior reporting and investigations into pension spiking in New York public-sector retirement systems. In 2010, Attorney General Cuomo launched a formal investigation into "pension padding" — cases where officers' total pay more than doubled in their final year. The Empire Center for Public Policy has repeatedly documented Suffolk County police compensation routinely exceeding $200,000 with overtime, feeding pensions that frequently exceed $100,000. In December 2024, a single retiring Rochester detective averaged 26 hours of overtime per week in his final year, grossed $299,033, and retired with a $169,176 annual pension — contributing to a 57% single-year spike in that city's police pension contributions.

This issue is now actively in front of state lawmakers. As part of "Fix Tier 6" budget proposals, state lawmakers are debating whether to lift the existing 15% OT cap for Tier 5 and 6 members. If enacted, this analysis suggests that change would materially expand the fiscal exposure documented here to a new generation of retirees.

9. Caveats & limitations

Staffing shortages and post-pandemic OT: A significant share of OT growth reflects real staffing pressures. The DiD design controls for this, but if retirees are systematically assigned to overtime-heavy posts in their final years, the DiD may partially attribute structural role changes to individual choice.

Suffolk OT intensity underestimation: As noted in Section 3.3, several Suffolk officers peaked their OT before the formal spike window, meaning DiD estimates are conservative for that agency.

Retro pay: Some large single-year figures in 2022–23 may include retroactive contract payments. To the extent these are pensionable they legitimately inflate FAS, but preliminary review does not suggest this is a dominant driver.

Control group contamination: Active employees within 1–3 years of retirement are themselves likely already spiking, making all DiD estimates conservative.

Tier differences: Tier 5 and 6 members face a 15% OT cap. They are roughly 2% of this cohort and would not contribute meaningfully to the aggregate figures.

Name-matching: Of 483 retirees, 101 could not be reliably matched (~21%). If these unmatched retirees spike at similar rates, total fiscal exposure scales proportionally upward.
