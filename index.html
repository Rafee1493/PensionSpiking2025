1. Executive summary

Pension spiking — the strategic escalation of overtime earnings in the years immediately preceding retirement to inflate the Final Average Salary (FAS) — is a well-documented vulnerability in public-sector defined-benefit systems. This report presents quantitative evidence of systematic spiking in two New York PFRS agencies: the NYS Division of Police and the Suffolk County Police Department.

Using payroll records from 2018 to 2024 and published pension data, we construct an overtime intensity metric and apply a difference-in-differences (DiD) framework. The DiD design isolates individual pre-retirement behavior from broader staffing and overtime trends by benchmarking retirees against comparable active officers in the same agency and bargaining unit.

The analysis finds statistically meaningful evidence that pre-retirement officers ramp overtime intensity in their final years, well above the increase observed among active officers over the same period. The implied excess annual pension obligation is approximately $4.66 million per year for the 382 matched retirees — a figure that compounds to over $93 million in undiscounted 20-year liability for this cohort alone.

2. Background: the pension spiking mechanism

The New York Police and Fire Retirement System (PFRS) has four member tiers. Tiers 1 and 2 cover officers who joined before July 1, 2009. Tier 3 covers a brief window from July 2009 through March 2012. Tier 6 applies to officers hired on or after April 1, 2012. There is no Tier 4 or Tier 5 in PFRS — those tiers exist only in the separate Employees' Retirement System. The overwhelming majority of the 2024 retirees in this study joined under Tier 2.

Under PFRS, a member's pension equals their Final Average Salary multiplied by years of credited service and a tier-specific accrual rate. For Tier 2 members — the dominant tier in this cohort — FAS is the average of the three highest consecutive years of earnings, and overtime is included in FAS without any cap. Tier 6 members face a 15% of salary cap on pensionable overtime, but represent fewer than 2% of the 2024 retirees studied.

The incentive is direct and large: a Trooper with a $100,000 base salary who adds $40,000 per year in overtime during the three-year FAS window raises their FAS from $100,000 to $140,000. With 20 years of service, that translates to a pension uplift of roughly $20,000 per year for life.

The 20% anti-spiking rule (applicable to post-1971 Tier 2 members): if any year's earnings in the FAS window exceed the preceding year's earnings by more than 20%, the excess is excluded. This creates the strategic ramp — rather than spiking in a single final year, officers gradually escalate overtime over 3–4 years, staying within the 20% threshold each year while substantially inflating FAS. The individual trajectories in Section 7 illustrate this pattern clearly.

3. Data & methodology

3.1 Data sources

NYS Division of Police retirees: The 2024 NYSLRS PFRS pension roll was matched to the NYS payroll database by name. Of 320 State Police entries, 247–252 were reliably matched to payroll records covering 2018–2024. Employees with unresolvable name duplicates were excluded.

Suffolk County Police retirees: Suffolk County public payroll data — which reports gross annual pay rather than a base pay rate — was matched to the 2024 NYSLRS PFRS pension roll. 135 Suffolk retirees were matched with sufficient payroll coverage; four to five with duplicate name matches were excluded.

Control groups: Active employees in both agencies, filtered to the same base pay ranges and bargaining units as the retiring cohort, were retained as controls. The State control group comprised 2,785 active officers; Suffolk comprised 1,344.

3.2 Overtime intensity metric and spike window definition

We normalize overtime using the overtime intensity ratio: annual overtime earnings divided by the officer's annualized base pay rate for that year. Three-year averages are computed for a baseline window (2018–2020) and a spike window. The spike window is defined differently depending on when the officer retired:

  • Officers retiring in the second half of 2024 (July–December): spike window is 2022–2024, with 2024 OT annualized.
  • Officers retiring in the first half of 2024 (January–June): spike window is 2021–2023, since the FAS window for these officers is more likely to include 2021 than 2024.

The year 2021 is excluded from analysis where appropriate for this reason. For the control group, the 2022–2024 window is used uniformly. Only officers with non-zero overtime in both periods are included in the DiD.

3.3 Suffolk County gross pay adjustment

Suffolk County payroll data reports gross annual pay — total compensation including overtime and other supplements — rather than a fixed base rate. This creates a subtle underestimation of spiking: some Suffolk officers worked their highest overtime several years before retirement (for example, Michael Malerba earned $145,654 in overtime in 2021 and $101,387 in 2019, but just $6,510 in 2022–23), which depresses their spike-window intensity while inflating their gross pay in those earlier years. The 2024 or 2023 gross pay figure would artificially reflect those peak-OT years as the "base," causing the pension-to-pay comparison to understate the pension premium. To correct for this, when comparing Suffolk pensions against pay in Section 6.2, we use each officer's average gross pay over 2021–2023 as the baseline rather than a single year's figure.

What the results show

After applying the corrected spike window — using 2021–2023 for H1 retirees and 2022–2024 for H2 retirees — the DiD estimates for State Police are modestly revised downward compared to the initial analysis, reflecting that some retirees' peak OT fell just outside the strict 2022–2024 window. Nonetheless, the pattern remains consistent and meaningful across all three State Police bargaining units.

The Comm & Non-Comm group shows the strongest State Police DiD at 34 percentage points — notable because their control group's OT growth was low (22%), making the retirees' 55% increase difficult to attribute to environmental factors alone. Investigators and Troopers each show a 22-point DiD gap, with retirees growing OT intensity at 83–84% versus 61–63% for active officers in those units.

In Suffolk County, the PBA patrol officers continue to show the strongest spiking signal at 28 points. The SDA detectives remain the one subgroup where no spiking is detectable — active detectives outpaced retirees by 8 points, consistent with a structural market trend rather than individual pre-retirement behavior. The SOA supervisor group shows a 21-point gap.

Five of six bargaining units show positive DiD estimates, three at or above 21 percentage points. Importantly, these estimates are conservative: as noted in Section 3.3, several Suffolk officers like Michael Malerba peaked their overtime years before the formal spike window, meaning their intensity ratios in 2022–24 actually understate the true magnitude of their lifetime OT escalation relative to baseline.

6.2 Pensions vs. pay: how many retirees earn more in pension than salary?

For State Police, each retiree's pension is compared against their 2023 base pay rate — a clean single-rate figure. For Suffolk County, pensions are compared against each officer's average gross pay over 2021–2023. This adjustment is necessary because Suffolk payroll reports gross compensation (inclusive of overtime), not a fixed pay rate. Officers like Michael Malerba, who earned very high overtime in 2019–2021 but far less in 2022–23, would show an artificially low 2023 gross pay that overstates the pension premium. Using the 2021–2023 average provides a more stable and representative earnings baseline.

Even with this conservative adjustment, 58 of 135 Suffolk retirees (43%) collect an annual pension exceeding their average 2021–23 gross pay. For State Police, 38 of 252 retirees (15%) collect pensions above their 2023 base pay rate.

8. Contextual benchmarks

This analysis is not an isolated anomaly. Pension spiking via overtime is a recognized, recurring pattern in New York public-sector retirement systems. In 2010, Attorney General Cuomo launched a formal investigation into "pension padding," noting cases where officers' total pay more than doubled in the final year and that a $50,000 average salary inflation over a 20-year payout could cost taxpayers $500,000 or more per individual. The Empire Center for Public Policy has repeatedly documented Suffolk County detectives averaging over $225,000 in total annual compensation including overtime, feeding pensions routinely exceeding $100,000. In Rochester (December 2024), a single retiring detective averaged 26 hours of overtime per week in his final year, grossed $299,033, and retired with a $169,176 annual pension — contributing to a 57% single-year spike in that city's police pension contributions. At the state level, newly retired NYPD officers averaged $103,859 in pension payments for FY2025, with the Empire Center attributing part of that increase to pandemic-era overtime inflating FAS values.

9. Caveats & limitations

Staffing shortages and post-pandemic OT: A significant share of the observed increase reflects real staffing pressures. The DiD design controls for this, but if retirees are systematically assigned to overtime-heavy posts in their final years, the DiD may partially attribute structural role changes to individual pre-retirement choice.

Suffolk OT intensity underestimation: As noted in Section 3.3, several Suffolk officers peaked their overtime before the formal spike window. Their 2022–24 intensity ratios understate the full lifetime escalation, meaning our DiD estimates are conservative for the Suffolk cohort.

Retro pay: Some large single-year figures in 2022–23 may include retroactive contract payments. To the extent these are pensionable they legitimately inflate FAS, but preliminary review does not suggest this is the dominant driver.

Control group contamination: Active employees within 1–3 years of retirement are likely already spiking, making all DiD estimates conservative.

Tier differences: Tier 6 members (post-2012, ~2% of this cohort) face a 15% OT cap. The effect on aggregate figures is negligible.

Name-matching: Approximately 20–25% of the pension roll could not be reliably matched to payroll records and are excluded, which may introduce selection bias.
