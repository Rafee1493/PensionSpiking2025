1. Executive summary

Pension spiking — the strategic escalation of overtime earnings in the years immediately preceding retirement to inflate the Final Average Salary (FAS) — is a well-documented vulnerability in public-sector defined-benefit systems. This report presents quantitative evidence of systematic spiking in two New York PFRS agencies: the NYS Division of Police and the Suffolk County Police Department.

Using payroll records from 2018 to 2024 and published pension data, we construct an overtime intensity metric and apply a difference-in-differences (DiD) framework. The DiD design isolates individual pre-retirement behavior from broader staffing and overtime trends by benchmarking retirees against comparable active officers in the same agency and bargaining unit.

The analysis finds statistically meaningful evidence that pre-retirement officers ramp overtime intensity in their final three years, well above the increase observed among active officers over the same period. The implied excess annual pension obligation is approximately $4.66 million per year for the 382 matched retirees — a figure that compounds to over $93 million in undiscounted 20-year liability for this cohort alone.

2. Background: the pension spiking mechanism

The New York Police and Fire Retirement System (PFRS) has four member tiers. Tiers 1 and 2 cover officers who joined before July 1, 2009. Tier 3 covers a brief window from July 2009 through March 2012. Tier 6 applies to officers hired on or after April 1, 2012. There is no Tier 4 or Tier 5 in PFRS (those tiers exist only in the separate Employees' Retirement System). The overwhelming majority of the 2024 retirees in this study — 313 of 320 State Police retirees — joined under Tier 2.

Under PFRS, a member's pension equals their Final Average Salary multiplied by years of credited service and a tier-specific accrual rate. For Tier 2 members — the dominant tier in this cohort — FAS is the average of the three highest consecutive years of earnings, and overtime is included in FAS without any cap. Tier 6 members face a 15% of salary cap on pensionable overtime, but they represent fewer than 2% of the 2024 retirees studied here.

The incentive is direct and large: a Trooper with a $100,000 base salary who adds $40,000 per year in overtime during the three-year FAS window raises their FAS from $100,000 to $140,000. With 20 years of service, that translates to a pension uplift of roughly $20,000 per year for life.

The 20% anti-spiking rule (applicable to post-1971 Tier 2 members): if any year's earnings in the FAS window exceed the preceding year's earnings by more than 20%, the excess is excluded. This creates the strategic ramp: rather than spiking in a single final year, officers gradually escalate overtime over 3–4 years, staying within the 20% threshold each year while still substantially inflating FAS. 3. Data & methodology

3.1 Data sources

NYS Division of Police retirees: The 2024 NYSLRS PFRS pension roll was matched to the NYS payroll database by name. Of 320 State Police entries, 247 were reliably matched to payroll records covering 2018–2024. Employees with unresolvable name duplicates were excluded.

Suffolk County Police retirees: Suffolk County public payroll data was matched to the 2024 NYSLRS PFRS pension roll. 135 Suffolk retirees were matched with sufficient payroll coverage; four to five with duplicate name matches were excluded.

Control groups: Active employees in both agencies were retained as controls, filtered to the same base pay ranges and bargaining units as the retiring cohort. The State control group comprised 2,785 active officers; the Suffolk control group comprised 1,344 officers.

3.2 Overtime intensity metric

We normalize overtime using the overtime intensity ratio: annual overtime earnings divided by the officer's annualized base pay rate. A ratio of 0.30 means overtime equivalent to 30% of base salary. This removes the mechanical inflation of OT dollars due to base pay raises and enables cross-agency, cross-year comparisons. Three-year averages are computed for the baseline window (2018–2020) and the spike window (2022–2024). The year 2021 is excluded from both analytical windows: officers retiring in early or mid-2024 may have had 2021 earnings included in their FAS, making 2021 a potential spiking year that would understate the baseline if included. Partial-year 2024 overtime for retirees is annualized. Only officers with non-zero overtime in both periods are included.

3.3 Difference-in-differences design

The DiD estimator asks: did retirees' overtime intensity increase more than comparable active officers' intensity over the same period? The parallel trends assumption is supported by both groups facing identical labor market conditions, COVID staffing pressures, and contract cycles.

Conservative bias in the control group: Active employees who are 1–3 years from retirement are themselves likely already spiking. Their inclusion in the control group means our DiD estimates understate the true magnitude of the spiking behavior — the actual gap between genuine non-spikers and retirees is larger than shown.

What the results show

The most striking finding is the Troopers bargaining unit, where retiring officers increased their average overtime intensity by 114% between the two windows — nearly double the 63% increase seen among active Troopers in the same period. The DiD gap of 51 percentage points is the largest of any subgroup. For Investigators, the DiD gap is 41 percentage points — retirees grew OT intensity at 102% versus 61% for active officers.

The Comm & Non-Comm group shows a 35-point DiD gap; notable because the control group's OT growth was relatively modest at 22%, suggesting less ambient staffing pressure in that subgroup, which makes the retirees' 57% growth harder to explain as anything other than deliberate spiking.

In Suffolk County, PBA patrol officers show the clearest spiking signal: a 28-point DiD gap, with retirees at 103% growth versus 75% for active officers. The Suffolk Detectives Association (SDA) is the single subgroup where no spiking is detectable — the active group actually outgrew retirees by 5 points, consistent with the broader market trend for detective roles rather than targeted pre-retirement behavior. The SOA supervisors show a moderate 21-point gap.

Together, five of six bargaining units show positive DiD estimates, three of which are above 28 percentage points. This pattern is not consistent with a random or environmental explanation; it is consistent with systematic pre-retirement overtime escalation.

Overtime earns outsized fiscal significance not as a one-time payroll cost but as a permanent uplift to FAS — which then pays out for 20 to 30 years. 6.2 Pensions vs. 2023 base pay: how many retirees earn more in pension than they did in salary?

Comparing each retiree's annual pension against their 2023 base pay rate provides a direct illustration of overtime's role: a pension exceeding base pay is only possible if substantial overtime was folded into FAS. Using 2023 base pay avoids the distortion of partial-year 2024 rates for officers who retired mid-year.

For Suffolk County, 59 of 135 retirees (44%) collect a pension higher than their 2023 base pay rate. For NYS State Police, 38 of 247 (15%) do. The gap between the two agencies reflects Suffolk's significantly higher overtime culture, where PBA officers routinely earned overtime approaching or exceeding base salary in the final years.

8. Contextual benchmarks

This analysis is not an isolated anomaly. Pension spiking via overtime is a recognized pattern in New York public-sector retirement systems. In 2010, Attorney General Cuomo launched a formal investigation into "pension padding," noting cases where officers' total pay more than doubled in the final year and that a $50,000 average salary inflation over a 20-year payout could cost taxpayers $500,000 or more per individual. The Empire Center for Public Policy has repeatedly documented Suffolk County detectives averaging over $225,000 in total annual compensation including overtime, feeding pensions routinely exceeding $100,000. In Rochester (December 2024), a single retiring detective averaged 26 hours of overtime per week in his final year, grossed $299,033, and retired with a $169,176 annual pension — contributing to a 57% single-year spike in that city's police pension contributions. At the state level, newly retired NYPD officers averaged $103,859 in pension payments for FY2025, with the Empire Center attributing a significant portion to pandemic-era overtime inflating FAS values.

9. Caveats & limitations

Staffing shortages and post-pandemic OT: A significant share of the observed increase reflects real staffing pressures. The DiD design controls for this by benchmarking against active officers. However, if retirees are systematically assigned to overtime-heavy posts in their final years, the DiD may partially attribute structural role changes to individual choice.

Retro pay: Some large single-year OT figures in 2022–23 may include retroactive contract payments. To the extent these are pensionable, they legitimately inflate FAS. Preliminary review of individual high earners does not suggest this is the dominant driver.

Control group contamination: Active employees within 1–3 years of retirement are likely already spiking, making our DiD estimate conservative.

Tier differences: Tier 6 members (hired post-2012, ~2% of this cohort) face a 15% OT cap. The analysis does not separate them; for this cohort the effect is negligible.

Name-matching: Approximately 20–25% of the pension roll could not be reliably matched to payroll records and are excluded, which may introduce selection bias.

10. Suggestions for further investigation

Expanding to earlier retiree cohorts (2020–2023) would allow a longitudinal test of whether spiking intensity has increased over time and would multiply the cumulative fiscal impact estimate. Expanding scope to other PFRS-member agencies — Nassau County Police, NYC Fire Department, major municipal departments — would provide a broader picture. A tier-specific breakdown would test whether uncapped Tier 2 members show larger DiD estimates than Tier 6 members, directly informing legislative reform proposals. Replacing the flat 20-year estimate with an NPV calculation incorporating actuarial life tables and COLA assumptions would sharpen the fiscal cost figure. Finally, if shift-assignment data are obtainable, testing whether pre-retirement officers are disproportionately assigned to overtime-rich roles would sharpen the causal attribution.
