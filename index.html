1. Executive summary

Pension spiking — the strategic escalation of overtime earnings in the years immediately preceding retirement to inflate the Final Average Salary (FAS) upon which pensions are calculated — is a well-documented vulnerability in public-sector defined-benefit systems. This report presents quantitative evidence of systematic spiking in two New York State Police and Fire Retirement System (PFRS) agencies: the NYS Division of Police and the Suffolk County Police Department.

Using payroll records from 2018 to 2024 and published pension data, we construct an overtime intensity metric (annual overtime earnings divided by base pay rate) and apply a difference-in-differences (DiD) framework. The DiD design cleanly separates individual pre-retirement behavior from broader environmental overtime trends — including COVID-related staffing surges and post-pandemic backlogs — by benchmarking retirees against a control group of comparable active officers.

The analysis finds statistically meaningful evidence that pre-retirement officers systematically ramp overtime intensity in the three years before they retire, over and above the increases observed in the active officer control group. The implied excess annual pension obligation arising from this spiking is estimated at approximately $4.7 million per year for the 384 matched retirees studied — a figure that compounds to over $93 million in undiscounted 20-year liability.

2. Background: the pension spiking mechanism

Under PFRS, a member's pension is a function of their Final Average Salary (FAS) and years of service. For Tier 1–5 members, FAS is the average of the three highest consecutive years of earnings. Tier 6 members (hired after April 2012) use the five highest consecutive years. Overtime pay is pensionable — it counts toward FAS — subject to a 15% of salary cap for Tier 5 and 6 members. Critically, the large majority of PFRS police members hired before 2012 face no overtime cap, meaning their entire overtime earnings in the FAS window affect their pension.

The incentive is direct: if a Trooper with a $100,000 base salary can add $40,000 per year in overtime during their final three years, their FAS rises from $100,000 to $140,000. With 20 years of service at a ~50% accrual, that translates to a pension of $70,000 instead of $50,000 — an additional $20,000 per year, for life.

The 20% anti-spiking rule exists for Tiers post-1971: if any year's earnings in the FAS window exceed the prior year by more than 20%, the excess is excluded. This creates the spiking strategy: officers do not wait until the final year, but ramp overtime gradually over 3–4 years to stay under the 20% cap, inflating FAS without triggering exclusions.

3. Data & methodology

3.1 Data sources

NYS Division of Police retirees: 2024 NYSLRS PFRS pension roll was matched to the NYS payroll database by name. Out of 320 State Police entries on the pension roll, 249 were reliably matched to payroll records covering 2018–2024. Annual overtime earnings and base pay rates were extracted for each matched employee for each year from 2018 to 2024, excluding 2021 (see below).

Suffolk County Police retirees: Suffolk County's public payroll data was matched to the 2024 NYSLRS PFRS pension roll. 135 of 163 Suffolk County Police retirees were matched with sufficient payroll coverage. Four to five were excluded due to duplicate name matches that could not be reliably disambiguated.

Control groups: Active employees in both agencies with at least one year of payroll history were used as controls. To ensure comparability, only active employees within the same base pay range as the retiring cohort — sliced by bargaining unit — were retained. This removes potential seniority confounds (senior officers earn more and may work different overtime patterns). The State control group comprised 2,785 employees across the three key bargaining units; the Suffolk County control group comprised 1,344 employees.

3.2 Overtime intensity metric

Raw overtime dollars are not directly comparable across officers with different base salaries, years, or agencies. We normalize overtime using the overtime intensity ratio: annual overtime earnings divided by the officer's annualized base pay rate in that year. A ratio of 0.30 means an officer earned overtime equivalent to 30% of their base salary. This normalization:

  • Removes the mechanical inflation of OT dollars due to base pay increases
  • Makes comparisons across different salary grades meaningful
  • Is stable enough to permit time-series comparisons

We compute three-year average intensity for two windows: 2018–2020 (pre-period, representing baseline behavior) and 2022–2024 (post-period, representing retirement-run behavior). The year 2021 is excluded from both windows. Officers who retire in early or mid-2024 may have had 2021 overtime included in their FAS calculation, making 2021 a potential spiking year for those individuals — including it in the baseline would understate the spiking effect.

The 2024 overtime for retirees who did not work the full year is annualized (divided by months of service in 2024 and multiplied by 12) to place it on a comparable annual basis.

Only officers who earned non-zero overtime in both periods are included in the DiD analysis, ensuring we analyze workers with genuine overtime exposure rather than those structurally excluded from overtime.

3.3 Difference-in-differences design

The DiD estimator asks: did retirees' overtime intensity increase more than the comparable active officers' intensity over the same period? The parallel trends assumption — that in the absence of spiking behavior, retirees and active officers would have experienced similar overtime growth — is supported by the fact that both groups were subject to the same labor market conditions, COVID-related staffing pressures, and contract cycles.

Key nuance on the control group: Active employees who are 1–2 years from retirement are themselves likely already spiking. Including them in the control group understates the true difference between spikers and non-spikers, making our DiD estimate conservative. The true scale of spiking is likely higher than reported here.

6. Pension cost impact

Overtime earns its outsized fiscal impact not just as a one-time payroll cost, but as a permanent uplift to the pension FAS — which then pays out for the rest of the retiree's life, often 20 to 30 years.

6.1 How excess overtime inflates pensions

The PFRS pension formula is roughly: FAS × service years × accrual rate. For Tiers 2–5 (the dominant tiers among 2024 retirees), FAS is the average of the three highest consecutive years. Overtime earned in those years enters FAS at full value (no cap for pre-Tier-5 members). When officers spike overtime, they directly inflate every dollar of their lifetime pension payments.

It is a conservative estimate because it does not account for COLA escalation or the fact that higher-tier pensions may have higher accrual multipliers.

Compounding effect: A $50,000 average salary spike in the FAS window, applied over a 20-year payout with a 50% accrual, translates to a $500,000+ taxpayer cost per individual — an illustrative figure consistent with prior AG investigations into pension padding. That figure was cited in the 2010 AG Cuomo investigation and remains directionally accurate for the retirees in our sample.

6.2 Pensions exceeding base pay: the clearest indicator

Perhaps the starkest illustration of overtime's role is the share of retirees whose pension annuity exceeds their final base pay rate — something impossible without substantial overtime being folded into FAS.

96% of Suffolk County retirees and 16% of State Police retirees collect pensions that exceed their base pay.

For Suffolk County, 130 out of 135 matched retirees collect an annual pension that exceeds their final base salary. This is largely explained by Suffolk's historically high overtime culture — county detectives' average total compensation including overtime routinely exceeds $225,000, and the pension FAS reflects that — but it also underscores that the pension system is paying out based on a total compensation figure that no future officer will sustain as a "normal" baseline.

8. Contextual benchmarks

This analysis is not an isolated anomaly. Pension spiking via overtime is a recognized, recurring pattern in New York public-sector retirement systems:

  • In 2010, Attorney General Andrew Cuomo launched a formal investigation into "pension padding" among New York local government employers, citing cases where officers' final-year total pay more than doubled through overtime, leading to six-figure pensions. The AG noted that $50,000 in salary inflation over a 20-year payout horizon could cost taxpayers $500,000 or more per individual.
  • The Empire Center for Public Policy has repeatedly documented Suffolk County detectives' average total compensation (including OT) exceeding $225,000, and noted that this feeds directly into pensions that routinely exceed $100,000 per year — well above national norms.
  • In Rochester (December 2024), a single retiring detective averaged 26 hours of overtime per week in his final year, grossing $299,033 and retiring with a $169,176 pension. Rochester's pension bill spiked 57% in a single year as post-pandemic OT inflated FAS values.
  • The NYPD spent over $1 billion in overtime in FY2025 — one-third of NYC's entire overtime budget — and the pension fund's payments to newly retired NYPD members averaged $103,859, up from prior years. The Empire Center attributed part of this increase to pandemic-era OT inflating FAS values.

New York State's Tier 6 reform (2012) capped overtime at 15% of salary for new members, but the majority of 2024 retirees were hired before 2012 and face no such cap. The reform will only begin to materially reduce pension spiking risk as the Tier 6 cohort approaches retirement in the 2030s.

9. Caveats & limitations

9.1 Staffing shortages and post-pandemic OT demand

A significant share of the OT increase observed in both the retiree and active cohorts from 2020 onward reflects real staffing pressures — vacancies, COVID-related absences, and court backlogs. The DiD design explicitly accounts for this by benchmarking against active officer trends. However, if retirees are systematically assigned to higher-OT duties (e.g., special events, overtime-rich posts) during their final years, the DiD may still attribute some structural role-change effects to spiking behavior. This would lead to a slight overestimate of volitional spiking.

9.2 Retro pay

Some large single-year OT figures — particularly in 2022 and 2023 — may include retroactive contract payments covering prior periods. To the extent these are genuinely pensionable (they are pensionable if reported as regular earnings), they would legitimately inflate FAS without representing "new" overtime hours. Preliminary inspection of individual high earners in the data does not suggest retro pay is the dominant driver, but this cannot be ruled out for individual cases.

9.3 Control group contamination

As noted, active employees who are 1–3 years from retirement are themselves likely spiking. Their inclusion in the control group makes the DiD estimate conservative — the true gap between spikers and genuine non-retirees is likely larger than reported.

9.4 Tier differences

OT caps apply to Tier 5 and 6 members; earlier tiers are uncapped. The 2024 retiree cohort is dominated by pre-Tier-6 members, for whom overtime is fully pensionable. As the Tier 6 cohort grows, this dynamic will gradually change.

9.5 Name-matching limitations

Pension records and payroll records were matched by name. A small number of retirees (roughly 20–25% of the initial pension roll) could not be reliably matched — typically due to name format inconsistencies or common names with multiple payroll records. These unmatched individuals are excluded from the analysis, which may introduce selection bias if unmatched retirees have systematically different OT patterns.

10. Recommendations for further investigation

  • Expand to earlier retirement cohorts: Analyzing 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023 retirees would allow a time-series analysis of whether spiking intensity has increased over the years, and would substantially widen the fiscal impact estimate.
  • Expand to other PFRS-member agencies: The methodology applies directly to any agency with PFRS members. Nassau County Police, NYC Fire Department, and large municipal police departments are natural next subjects.
  • Tier-specific analysis: Breaking out Tier 2–4 vs. Tier 5 members would test whether the uncapped tiers show higher spiking magnitude — a direct policy-relevant question for any legislative reform proposal.
  • Supervisor shift assignment data: If shift and assignment records are obtainable, analysis of whether pre-retirement officers are assigned to overtime-rich roles disproportionately would sharpen the causal attribution.
  • COLA-adjusted lifetime liability: The 20-year undiscounted estimate should be replaced with a net present value calculation incorporating COLA escalation and actuarial life expectancy by agency and bargaining unit.
